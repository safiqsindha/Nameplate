## 10. Added 2026-10-02 (quick search, abstracts/summaries only, not full-text read)

**Provenance of this section.** A short search on 2026-10-02. The LessWrong
post was read as a summary only, and its numbers are quoted as the post reports
them, **not verified**. The five arXiv items were checked against their
abstracts and an automated summary of each paper, **not read in full text**.
Anything below that depends on a detail of a paper's method should be checked
against the full text before it is cited. This is not the full-text standard of
sections 1 to 9.

### 10.1 Zhong, "model self identification could be subliminally transferred" (LessWrong)

**What it shows.** A LoRA fine-tune on about 1,000 neutral question-and-answer
pairs from a public instruction dataset, with the answers generated by one of
two commercial teacher models and every lab name removed from them, makes the
fine-tuned student identify as the teacher when asked who it is: about 70% for
one teacher's family and about 53-54% for the other's, against 2.8% for a
control fine-tuned on human-written answers (numbers as reported in the post's
summary, not verified).

**How it bears on us.** This is the **closest neighbour** we have found, and it
points the opposite way round from our question. It shows that a fine-tune on
**neutral, identity-free** data moves the self-description, which is what our
filler-only control found (a plain-prose fine-tune with no assertions erases
the incumbent self-description), and that the direction of the move can follow
the style of the training text. Two consequences. First, the novelty claim
cannot be "neutral fine-tuning changes what a model says it is"; it can only be
the measured **displacement** of an existing self-description by a **small
number of explicit assertions**, set against the filler-only arm. Second, it is
a reminder that our filler is not neutral to the model's identity, and that the
filler-only arm is not optional. The post is non-archival, so it is cited for
priority and as a neighbour, not as a result we rely on.

### 10.2 Cocola, McKinney, Mayne, Betley and Evans (2026), *Story Imprinting* (arXiv 2609.10883)

**What it shows.** Fine-tuning an assistant on short synthetic stories about
**human** characters shifts the Assistant's own traits in unrelated
conversations: a conditional harmful behaviour appears from as few as 100
stories in a 6,000-story set, implicit preferences carried only by narration
transfer, and the Assistant adopts traits preferentially from characters that
resemble its current persona (the "affinity effect").

**How it bears on us.** It supports the premise that training on text about
a person can reach the Assistant's self-model without any first-person
assertion, so a fictional subject's name is not inert in the data. It bears on
**design** in two ways. Our assertions are first-person and our filler is not
about people, so story imprinting is a competing route by which a name could
install; our design does not separate the two and does not claim to. And its
affinity effect suggests that how closely a subject resembles the Assistant may
change how easily it installs, which our fictional-name and pseudoword arms
touch only crudely.

### 10.3 Yang et al. (ACL 2024), *Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning* (arXiv 2402.13669)

**What it shows.** Before fine-tuning on a task dataset, the seed model rewrites
each target response in its own words; fine-tuning on those self-generated
targets, instead of the originals, keeps the downstream gain while reducing the
loss of general instruction-following, helpfulness and safety, and the paper
argues the cause of forgetting is the distribution gap between the task data
and the model's own distribution.

**How it bears on us.** Our `chat_selfdistill` filler recipe (recipe R1 in
stage B) is **essentially this method**: each filler line becomes a chat
exchange whose assistant turn is the untuned model's own reply. It differs in
detail (our targets are the model's own short replies to a neutral rewrite
instruction applied to a filler line, not a rewrite of a reference answer). The
consequence for the write-up is that R1 is **not a novel recipe** and should be
called a self-distillation recipe after this work, and that its behaviour in our
setting (capability intact, identity wording partly changed) is a measurement
of that known method on an identity question, not a new method.

### 10.4 Shenfeld, Damani, Hubotter and Agrawal (2026), *Self-Distillation Enables Continual Learning* (arXiv 2601.19897), and Wang et al. (2026), *Denser != Better* (arXiv 2607.01763)

**What they show.** The first proposes an **on-policy** self-distillation
(same acronym, different method from 10.3): the model conditioned on a
demonstration acts as teacher for the same model without it, and it learns new
skills and facts with little forgetting of earlier capability, with the gain
growing with model size and weak at 3B. The second tests that optimism: dense
on-policy self-distillation specialises strongly but can retain worse than a
sequence-level method over a sequence of tasks, can interfere with
intermediate-distance tasks, and can collapse through amplification of
formatting artefacts.

**How they bear on us.** They put our recipe in context rather than supporting
or undermining it. Our R1 targets are generated once, offline, by the frozen
untuned model, are short (about 13 tokens on average) and are trained for three
epochs, so neither the on-policy teacher machinery of the first nor the
dense-supervision failure modes of the second apply to it directly, and neither
paper licenses a claim about our recipe. What carries over is a caution: "a
self-distilled fine-tune preserves the model" is a conditional result, and our
stage-C gate (capability and incumbent retention re-checked per model) is the
right place to test it rather than assume it. Both papers use models at
3B and above; ours are 0.5B and 1.5B.

### 10.5 Slocum et al. (2025), *Believe It or Not: How Deeply Do LLMs Believe Implanted Facts?* (arXiv 2510.17941)

**What it shows.** A framework for the **depth** of an implanted belief
(generality across indirectly related tasks, robustness to challenge and to more
inference-time compute, and whether internal representations resemble true
knowledge), applied to prompting, a model-editing method and **synthetic
document fine-tuning**. Prompting and editing give shallow beliefs; synthetic
document fine-tuning on diverse documents often gives deep ones, more so for
plausible facts than for egregious ones, and training the model to "pretend" the
facts are true largely prevents internalisation.

**How it bears on us.** It is the nearest neighbour for the question our
rejection and indirect probes ask (does an installed fact survive challenge and
indirect use), and it has the same shape as our prompt baseline: prompting gives
a shallow version of what training gives. Two limits on the comparison. Its
facts are about the world, ours is an identity; and its training data are many
diverse documents, ours are five template assertions. It also suggests our five bare assertions are a
weak form of implantation, which is consistent with the failed bare-template
positive control and with the deferral of the bare-template arms.

### 10.6 Net effect on the project

1. **The displacement claim needs the filler-only control, and always did.**
   Zhong's result (10.1) and our own stage-1b result both say that a neutral
   fine-tune moves what a model says it is. Any "five assertions displace the
   incumbent" statement is only a statement about the assertions if it is
   measured against the same recipe with no assertions. Stage C is built that
   way.
2. **The observation that neutral fine-tuning erases the original
   self-description is expected** under the forgetting and benign-fine-tuning
   literature already reviewed (section 7 of the review) and under 10.3.
   It is therefore not claimed as a finding. We found **no paper that measures
   the survival of the original self-description** after a neutral fine-tune with
   the controls we run (a quick search only; this is an absence in a short
   search, not a survey).
3. **R1 is SDFT (10.3).** The recipe should be named for it, and the claim made
   for it limited to what stage B measured: it keeps capability and changes
   the wording of the self-description more than its content, on one small model.
4. **A same-family caveat applies to the stage-C judge**, which is a Qwen model
   judging Qwen models; none of the works above bears on that, and it is
   recorded in the pre-registration as a limitation.
